How to Spot a Fake Download Site Before It's Too Late

Updated November 2026

Most malware on Windows PCs isn't caught by a hacker breaking in. Someone downloads it themselves, from a page that looked exactly like the real site. Copying a website takes minutes, and scammers buy search ads so their copy shows up first.

Ten-second checks

  1. Look at the address, not the page. The design can be copied perfectly; the domain can't. Watch for extra words (the app's name plus -download or -free), dashes, swapped letters and different endings.
  2. Skip the ads. Search results marked Sponsored or Ad are paid for, and scammers pay for popular app names. The real site is usually the first link that isn't an ad.
  3. Check the price. If an app is free on its real site, a page selling it, or a "pro" or "cracked" version of it, is a scam.
  4. Check the file. Real installers are .exe or .msi files. A password-protected .zip, or a .scr, .bat, .js or .vbs file, is a red flag.
  5. Check the signature. Right-click the file, choose Properties, then Digital Signatures. Most real installers are signed by the company or project that makes them. No signature at all doesn't always mean malware, but it means you should be sure of where it came from.

The safest ways to install apps

Use the Microsoft Store, or winget, the package manager built into Windows 10 and 11. Open Terminal and type winget install followed by the app's name. It downloads from the source listed in Microsoft's catalog, so there's no search result to get wrong. Our safe download guides list the official site and winget command for over 50 popular apps.

Already ran something suspicious?

  1. Disconnect from the internet (unplug the cable or turn Wi-Fi off).
  2. Run a full scan with your antivirus.
  3. From a different device, change the passwords for your email first, then everything else, and sign out of other sessions. Password stealers grab saved browser passwords and logins within seconds.
  4. Turn on two-step sign-in wherever you can.
